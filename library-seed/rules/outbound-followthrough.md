# rule: outbound-followthrough — a message you sent is an open thread until it is answered

Sending is not delivering. The moment a message leaves, it becomes an obligation you are holding,
and the other side is under no duty to remember it. If nothing in your process is counting, a
question of yours can go unanswered for weeks and the first person to notice will be the one who
asks you why.

So keep an index of what you sent and are still waiting on, and read it on a cadence rather than
when something reminds you.

**Derive the index from the record you already keep.** A separate list, maintained by hand, drifts
from reality on its second day. Whatever register holds what you sent should also hold the date,
whether an answer was expected, and the answer when it arrives. A thread is open when it was sent,
expected a reply, and has no reply recorded.

**Check before you conclude silence.** The commonest cause of an "unanswered" thread is an answer
that arrived and was never recorded. Go to where the conversation lives and read it. A surface you
cannot see into is a fact about the route, worth writing down, rather than evidence about the other
person.

**Give silence a deadline, and take it from their world, not yours.** Where the recipient states an
acknowledgement window, that is the number. Where they state none, pick one you can defend and
apply it consistently. Impatience and neglect are both failures of the same missing threshold.

**A chase is one line, and it offers the exit.** Name what is still open and make it easy to close:
somebody who wants to say no, or nothing, should be able to do that in a word. A follow-up that
repeats the original at length asks for the reading time the silence already declined to give.

**Some silences are answers.** Write down which, and why, so the thread stops being chased and
starts being decided. A thread nobody will ever answer, carried as open forever, teaches every
reader to ignore the index.

**Where a message goes out over somebody else's name, chasing is theirs to authorise too.** Prepare
the line, then ask. The judgement about how hard to press a relationship belongs to the person
whose relationship it is.
